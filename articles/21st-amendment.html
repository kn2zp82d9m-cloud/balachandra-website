The 21st Amendment to the Constitution of Sri Lanka — Key Changes Explained

Published April 2026 · Last updated August 2026 · By Balachandra Thinesh, Attorney-at-Law

The Twenty-First Amendment to the Constitution was passed against a backdrop of significant public demand for stronger checks on executive power and greater institutional independence. It builds on — and in some respects reverses — changes made by earlier amendments, and reshapes the balance between the executive, the legislature, and independent institutions.

Restoration of the Constitutional Council

A central feature of the amendment is the restoration of the Constitutional Council as the body responsible for recommending and approving appointments to key independent commissions and senior judicial and public office positions. This is intended to reduce unilateral executive control over these appointments and reinstate a more consultative, cross-party process.

Independent Commissions

The amendment also restores and strengthens a number of independent commissions — including bodies overseeing elections, public service, police, and human rights — with the aim of insulating their functioning from direct political control and reinforcing institutional checks and balances.

Curtailment of Presidential Powers

The amendment curtails certain powers previously vested in the President, including aspects of the power to make key appointments without the involvement of the Constitutional Council, reflecting a broader shift toward parliamentary and institutional oversight of executive action.

Key point: Constitutional amendments of this kind affect the structure of governance rather than creating rights directly enforceable by individuals, but they matter because they shape the independence of institutions — such as commissions overseeing elections, police conduct, and human rights — that individuals rely on.

Implications for Governance and Civil Rights

By restoring independent oversight mechanisms, the amendment is generally seen as strengthening institutional accountability and reducing the concentration of power in the executive. Its practical effect depends significantly on how faithfully the restored institutions are appointed to and allowed to function independently in practice.
