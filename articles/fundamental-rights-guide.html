Fundamental Rights Under the Sri Lankan Constitution — A Plain-Language Guide

Published June 2026 · By Balachandra Thinesh, Attorney-at-Law

The Constitution of the Democratic Socialist Republic of Sri Lanka sets out, in Chapter III, a list of fundamental rights guaranteed to every person. These are not abstract ideals — they are enforceable rights, and Article 126 gives the Supreme Court exclusive jurisdiction to hear complaints that these rights have been infringed by executive or administrative action. This article walks through what the key rights actually mean in practice, and how they can be enforced.

Equality Before the Law — Article 12

Article 12 guarantees that all persons are equal before the law and entitled to equal protection of the law. It also specifically prohibits discrimination on the grounds of race, religion, language, caste, sex, political opinion, place of birth, or any one of such grounds. In practice, this provision is frequently invoked to challenge arbitrary or discriminatory executive decisions, unequal treatment in public employment, and unfair administrative action.

Freedom From Torture — Article 11

No person shall be subjected to torture, or to cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment or punishment. This is one of the few rights in the Constitution that is not subject to any restriction, even during a state of emergency, reflecting its fundamental character.

Freedom From Arbitrary Arrest, Detention and Punishment — Article 13

Article 13 requires that a person arrested be informed of the reason for the arrest, and be brought before the nearest competent court according to procedure established by law. It also protects against retroactive criminal punishment and double jeopardy, and guarantees a fair trial by a competent court.

Freedom of Speech, Assembly, Association, and Movement — Article 14

Article 14 guarantees freedoms including speech and expression, peaceful assembly, association, movement within Sri Lanka, and the freedom to engage in any lawful occupation, profession, trade, business, or enterprise. These freedoms are subject to restrictions that may be imposed by law in the interests of matters such as national security, public order, and the rights of others.

How These Rights Are Enforced

A person who alleges that one of these rights has been infringed by executive or administrative action may petition the Supreme Court under Article 126. This is a strict-timeline remedy — the petition must generally be filed within one month of the date of the alleged infringement. The petition proceeds first to a leave-to-proceed stage, and if leave is granted, to a full hearing, which may result in declarations, compensation, or other appropriate relief.

Key point: Fundamental rights applications are only available against executive or administrative action — not against private individuals or purely legislative acts. Identifying the correct respondent and cause of action is essential, and this should be assessed promptly given the one-month time limit.

Why This Matters

Understanding these rights is the first step in recognising when they may have been infringed — whether through an unlawful arrest, an arbitrary administrative decision, or interference with lawful expression. Because of the strict procedural requirements and time limits involved, anyone who believes their rights have been infringed should seek legal advice as early as possible.
